Windpath / finding the businesses worth advising on, while there is still time

A quiet, public-data method for spotting owner-managed businesses that are heading towards a sale, a wind-down or a closure — early enough that a good outcome is still possible — and for reaching the owner through someone you already know.

Prepared for you · 7 November 2026 · UK registers are live in this demo; the US is a roadmap item · draft, not yet run on live mandates

In plain English

The problem. Most businesses don't close overnight. A year or two before it shows up anywhere obvious, the signs are already in the public record: accounts filed late, a sole director in their mid-sixties with nobody younger on the board, a new lender registering a charge. By the time it reaches an agent's listing or a court notice, someone else has usually been instructed and the value is draining away.

What Windpath does. It reads those public records for the kind of company you would want to advise and ranks them by how realistic a deal is — not by how distressed they look. Then it asks one question about each: who do you already know who could introduce you? If the answer is nobody, it doesn't suggest an approach. It tells you which accountant, lawyer or lender you should be meeting instead.

What it is sized for. One or two additional mandates a year, run by one person. Not a pipeline of dozens, and nothing that makes cold calls. The tool drafts and ranks; nothing is ever sent to an owner unless you decide to send it.

What you are being asked. A go / no-go on this direction. No price, no commitment. The last section sets out the three decisions that would let us scope a build.

Six words used throughout
Rung
How far along a business is on its way to closing (0 = quietly decided, nothing visible; 5 = dissolved).
Signal
A fact in a public register that tends to appear before a sale or closure — e.g. late accounts.
Gate
A rule that blocks a company before anyone is approached — e.g. "already in liquidation".
Warm path
A named person you know who knows the owner, or can introduce you.
Gatekeeper
An accountant, lawyer, lender or insolvency practitioner who sees situations early and can route them.
Fee floor
The smallest fee you'd take a mandate for. Below it the hours don't pay.
Visible, late end of the market
~23,000
Formal company insolvencies a year in England & Wales. By then, the adviser is already chosen.
Where big firms and agents appear
Rung 3–4
And only above a size that covers their minimum fee — roughly £5m enterprise value.
The whitespace
Rungs 0–2
No incumbent adviser, the owner still decides, and going-concern value still exists.

Why this is winnable

Large advisory firms and business-transfer agents generally arrive once a business is visibly for sale or insolvent, and only when the deal is big enough to cover their minimum fee. Below that size, and earlier in the sequence, there is no incumbent to displace and the owner still makes the decisions. The only way in is a relationship.

The competitive edge is not speed. It is stage — and a former-Big-4 adviser with a real network is exactly who gets the first call at that stage.

03Gates

Before any company is suggested for an approach it passes ten checks. Each returns PASS, WARN or REFUSE with a plain-English reason, and nothing is ever deleted — a refusal goes to a queue where a person decides. The checks are simple rules with no network, database or AI inside, so they are predictable and testable. Pick an example below (all fictional) or edit the record and watch the verdict change.

Live logic · gates G1–G10

REFUSE goes to the review queue with its reason. Never deleted.

HUMAN held for a person — for example a possible duplicate. A fuzzy name match alone never suppresses anything.

WARN proceed with care, for example below the fee floor (needs a signed waiver).

Only a person ever sends anything to an owner.

04Scoring

Six factors — Transferability, Inherent value, Timing, Accessibility, Size and Cleanliness, nicknamed TITASC — two of which are hard gates rather than scores. Weights are deliberately skewed to Transferability and Accessibility — the two things a solo operator cannot buy and cannot fix later. Inherent value and size can be worked; a non-assignable contract and a missing warm path cannot.

Sensitivity — which lever is worth pulling

Two levers matter and they are not the obvious ones. Warm-path coverage — doubling the universe adds work at every stage and barely moves closed deals; raising the proportion of targets with a named human route moves them directly. That is why the engine's output is a routing decision, not a list. And the capacity cap — once it binds, every upstream improvement is wasted until the fee floor rises or a second operator arrives. Watch which one binds before spending anything.

06Gatekeepers

You will not out-search a large firm. You can out-relationship one, because relationships do not scale and large-firm origination runs on sector coverage and brand — not on being the person a Barnsley accountant rings on a Friday afternoon. So invert the funnel: 40–60 gatekeepers who each hold 10–50 relevant situations, instead of 500 targets approached directly.

Model · ring structure and yield
Ring 1 is finite and non-renewable. Burning it on poor-fit targets is the failure mode that kills this kind of practice — not a lack of leads.

Warm-path routing protocol

1. WHO holds decision rights? owner / board / IP / PE / joint receivers 2. WHO do we know that knows them? 1st degree → 2nd degree → gatekeeper → none 3. ROUTE 1st or 2nd degree → through the named human, artefact attached gatekeeper only → brief the gatekeeper, let them make the intro none → DO NOT APPROACH. park it; work the ring instead 4. Every approach carries the counterfactual, not a pitch. 5. Every approach is logged with: trigger, evidence row, rung, archetype, route, person, date.

A target with no route is not a lead. It is evidence that a gatekeeper ring is missing — which is itself actionable, and is what the warm-path coverage metric measures.

The counterfactual — the actual door-opener

One artefact, built once, reused on every approach. An owner does not want to sell a business. They want to stop being tired, stop being scared, or stop losing money. The thing that earns the meeting is a rigorous number for the difference between the two futures.

If you close
  • Lease exit & dilapidations
  • Statutory redundancy, N staff
  • Supplier settlement
  • Assets at auction recovery rates
  • Personal guarantee exposure
  • Tax on chargeable gains
  • BBL / CBILS shortfall
  • Residual wrongful-trading risk
If you transact
  • Net consideration, each of options a–h
  • Less fees
  • Less retained liabilities
  • Plus deferred / earn-out value
  • Employees transferred
  • Liabilities left in the entity
  • Reputation intact
  • Speed to certainty

You can build this to a standard of rigour the owner has never seen applied to their own business, and that no agent's valuation email can match. It is also honest — sometimes the left column wins, and saying so is what earns the referral network.

08Under the bonnet

Detail for when you want to test the method rather than just see it. Everything here is collapsed by default.

Known pitfalls, written down before launch

These are the failure modes that tend to sink this kind of engine. Each has a rule that blocks it and a test that proves the rule works.

Three sources that don't behave as you'd expect
Watch-out

HR1 redundancy notices have no company names

The UK's strongest early trigger — a statutory 30-day advance notice of closure-scale job losses — is published only in aggregate: forms, potential redundancies and unique employers per month, with ONS adding industry and region. No employer names, anywhere, officially.

Approach: use the aggregate as a sector heat signal, then resolve named employers from Jobcentre Plus Rapid Response announcements, local press and union statements. Label the result inferred, never confirmed.

Watch-out

The best signal is filtered out upstream

ONS cleans the HR1 data by dropping any entry whose reason mentions transfer, move, or TUPE — because those aren't redundancies.

Approach: invert the filter. Those entries describe a business or contract moving to a new operator: a live transaction with a completion date that nobody is monitoring.

Edge

The insolvency stream names the gatekeeper

The Companies House streaming service pushes formal insolvency events in real time, free. The most valuable field isn't the company — it's the appointed insolvency practitioner.

Approach: two years of that stream builds a map of who actually gets appointed in your sector and region — assembled from evidence, not a directory.

Fee floor — what each deal size does to the practice
Registers: UK live, US as a roadmap

Companies House gives the UK a single national census of companies. The US has no equivalent, but its WARN Act publishes named employers 60 days ahead of closure-scale events — something the UK doesn't do at all. Only the UK is demonstrated here. The US register set is a later phase.

UK to US trigger parity

The officer date-of-birth signal exists only in the UK. It is what makes succession exposure (the largest and least-contested archetype) visible from a register; in the US that archetype has to be reached through accountants and lawyers instead.
What "adviser-side only" settles
Kept

Closure support stays on the menu. Helping an owner close properly is what makes you the person the accountant calls first. If you were also a buyer it would be a conflict and would have to go.

Works

Insolvency practitioners become a channel. An adviser who brings a going-concern outcome or a component buyer is useful to them. A principal would just be a competing purchaser.

Recommended

A standing conflicts check before every mandate: who is the buyer, have I met them, am I paid by anyone else in this? Component deals can otherwise put you on both sides of one asset.

09The decision

A go / no-go on direction. If it is a go, three things to confirm so a build can be scoped. No price is attached to any of them.

Decision 1

The mandate

Enterprise value £1m–£5m, with a written fee floor of £30,000 modelled fee. Revisit after twenty live outcomes. This is the band where large firms are structurally absent and the economics of a solo practice still work.

Decision 2

The scope

Adviser-side only. UK first. US registers stay a roadmap item until the UK engine has produced real introductions. One region and a handful of sectors to begin with.

Decision 3

The next step

A short scoping conversation to agree time and budget for a build, using your sectors and fee floor and a first look at your own network against the target list.
